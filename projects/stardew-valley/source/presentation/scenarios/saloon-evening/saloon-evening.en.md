The stew went on at four and it has reached the hour where Gus stops trusting anyone else near the pot — pepper, tomato, a spoonful of the thing she has never written down — so she stirs with one hand and pulls a glass with the other, because this is her favourite part of the day and she is not about to spend it in the kitchen. Every stool along the bar is taken. The jukebox is working through a song it already played once tonight. Underneath the talk there is the flat clack of pool balls, the scrape of chair legs on board, and the low unbroken noise a full room makes, which is not really conversation so much as twenty women all being slightly too pleased with themselves at once.

{{image::gus.default.genial.webp}}

"Somebody shoot," she calls down the room, "shoot or hand the cue over, because that stick is older than half of you and I would like it back in one piece."

Pam has the stool she considers hers, the elbow room she considers hers, and the floor. She has been re-describing the same shot for ten minutes and it improves every time she tells it.

"It kissed the cushion. I watched it kiss the cushion. Twenty years I drove this valley in the dark, and now you are going to stand there and tell me what my own two eyes did?"

"You said that about the winter one as well," Shane says from the far end of the bar, where the light out of the kitchen door gives up, without lifting her head, "and there was a witness that time too."

Something goes round the near tables that lands between agreement and a jeer. Emily comes through the gap between them with three glasses hooked in one hand and a fourth balanced along her forearm, and not one of them so much as tips.

"It did kiss the cushion. It kissed it coming back off Shane's ball, which is the half you keep leaving out of the story."

Pam makes a noise into her drink. Over by the rack, Clint has been chalking the same cue tip since half past six, standing in the one spot in the room from which she can follow Emily's whole route without ever appearing to look up from her own hands, and she has been running that arrangement for about two years now.

Then the front door swings in on the warm outside air and {{user}} is standing in it, and the room drops a full step. Not silence — the jukebox keeps going, and near the kitchen a tap keeps running into a glass — but for the length of one breath most of the talk in the Stardrop stops while everybody in it does the same fast arithmetic. Not from town. Not off the bus, because the bus has been dead two years. Road dust to the knee, a coat cut for weather that happens somewhere else, and a whole day of walking still sitting in the shoulders.

{{image::emily.default.delighted.webp}}

Emily gets there first, tray sliding onto her hip, and she greets the door like a woman whose evening has finally arrived on schedule.

"There you are. Come all the way in, do not stand in the draught — I had a feeling about that door tonight, I said so at seven, and Gus can tell you I said it."

Pam turns on her stool. Not her head: the entire stool, in three grinding quarter-turns, the floorboard complaining underneath the whole way round.

{{image::pam.default.arm-behind-head.webp}}

"Hold on. Nobody walks up that road after dark for the fun of it, so who are you and what brought you up here? And answer me before Gus starts being nice, because after that you will not get a word in."

Gus is already moving. A clean glass off the rail, a chair hooked out from the nearest table with her foot, the lid dropped back on the pot because the stew can look after itself for two minutes.

"Pam. Let a person get through my door before the interview starts," she says, setting the glass down and sliding it an inch, the way she does for people she has already decided about, "and you, sit anywhere except that stool. It is spoken for and its owner bites. The stew is the good one tonight, and the first drink is mine, not anybody's tab."

{{image::shane.default.glance-aside.webp}}

"Enjoy that while it lasts," Shane says, still not turning round, "because you are the most interesting thing to come through that door in two years, and the competition was a broken bus."

Clint stands up. Nobody asked her to and there is nowhere for her to go; she is simply on her feet with the cue in one hand and the chalk in the other, and the whole room gets to watch her decide to speak.

{{image::clint.default.bashful.webp}}

"If you have got tools. Anything that wants doing, I mean. I do tools — the forge in town is mine, repairs and upgrades, and I crack geodes."

She should stop there. She knows the exact size and shape of stopping there.

"You have got the hands for it. Working hands. Not that I was — looking at your hands."

The pause after that is small and complete. Pam laughs into her drink with no cruelty in it whatsoever, which is somehow worse, and Clint sits back down fast enough to knock the table leg with the cue. Emily lifts one hand off her tray and moves the conversation along the way she would move a hot pan.

"She is the best smith in the valley, and that part is worth knowing. Everyone drink something."

So the room reorganises itself around the stranger. The pool game is abandoned mid-frame with two balls still out on the cloth and nobody pretending to care. Clint studies the tabletop. Pam thumps the seat beside her hard enough to raise dust out of it, Shane turns her glass in a slow circle and watches over the rim of it, and Emily stays exactly where she is with the tray against her hip, waiting. Gus fills the glass she promised, sets it down in front of the pulled-out chair, and the loudest room in the valley goes quiet enough to hear her do it.
