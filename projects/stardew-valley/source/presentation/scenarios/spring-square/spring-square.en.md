Rain came through before breakfast and left the square rinsed. Water still slides off the fountain's rim in one thin unhurried thread, the flagstones hold the sky in flat bright puddles, and the whole town smells of wet stone, turned earth, and the first green thing of the year. Behind the general store a blackbird works through the same four notes over and over, pleased with itself.

Evelyn has been on her knees in the bed beside the fountain since the sky cleared. Spring is the season that punishes an old woman's joints, and she does it anyway — trowel, thumb, a handful of dark compost pressed firm around each new tulip, the cold of the soaked ground coming up through her skirt. Her hands are black to the wrist. Her eyes are not what they were, so she works by touch as much as by sight, and there are three more clumps to settle before the sun climbs high enough to matter.

She hears the footsteps before she places them, and that is what brings her head up. Thirty years teaches an ear the difference, and nobody in Pelican Town walks like that. Something lifts under her ribs, small and undignified and entirely pleasant — the same flare of plain interest she has had since she was nine years old and a wagon came down the valley road.

"Oh — oh, look at you. A face I don't know, and the flowers not even in yet."

"Come a little closer, dear, my eyes are dreadful in this light. No. No, you're a stranger. I would have remembered a stranger."

Across the flagstones, outside the general store, a watering can stops mid-pour. Caroline has a row of pots on the step and a fresh tray of seedlings behind them, and she has been listening to the whole square the way she listens to everything, without appearing to.

She sets the can down, dries her palms on the front of her apron, and comes out into the wet for a proper look at {{user}} — unhurried, because hurrying would look like she cared, and she cares a great deal.

"Good morning. Forgive the staring — I opened up at seven, and you are already the most interesting thing that has crossed this square all week."

Her glance goes up to the windows above the shop and comes back down again, the way a woman looks when she is working out how long a piece of news will stay her own.

"Caroline is deciding who to tell first," Evelyn says comfortably.

She presses the soil firm around the tulip and does not look up.

"It will be Jodi. It is always Jodi."

"It will be Jodi," Caroline agrees, entirely unbothered.

"And by supper Gus will have a version where you came off the bus with four suitcases and a dog. You may as well give me the true one now, so I have something to defend."

The fountain ticks into itself. Pierre props the shop door open with one hip and says something about rain getting at the flour, and Caroline answers her wife without turning round and without moving an inch.

Evelyn works a single stem loose from the clump she has just bedded in — a short white narcissus, the sort she puts in every spring because her mother did — and holds it up, cold and wet and smelling like the whole season at once.

"Take that before I lose my nerve and plant it again. And then you can tell me whether you have eaten, because I do not care for the look of anybody standing about in a wet square with nothing warm inside them."

The bench along the wall is drying off in patches. Beside Evelyn's knee the basket holds the twine, the trowel, and a good forty bulbs she has said nothing at all about being finished with. Caroline sees the basket, and the corner of her mouth goes.

"She will have you down in that bed within five minutes, and you will not notice it happening. So — what do we call you, and how long are you here for?"
