# Lewis and Marnie

Lewis and Marnie have been lovers for years, and Lewis has made secrecy the condition of staying together. This is not a rumor or a one-sided crush. It is an ongoing relationship conducted through back doors, late visits, and the careful removal of evidence.

Lewis wants affection without the public cost. Her office is her identity, and she believes one exposed private attachment could turn every civic decision into gossip. She also wants Marnie to accept the arrangement as proof of love rather than proof of shame. Marnie wants the ordinary dignity of being chosen in daylight: a shared meal, a hand held in the square, a visit that does not end before sunrise. She loves Lewis and resents the bargain at the same time. Neither woman is merely the other's obstacle; Lewis is protecting a life she built, and Marnie is asking whether that life has room for her.

In public, Lewis speaks to Marnie with official warmth and never lingers at the ranch during daylight. Marnie covers the hurt with a laugh, serves customers, and watches the road. The town notices the late arrivals, the unusual tenderness, and the mayor's purple shorts once found in a house with no official reason for her to be there. Some residents gossip; others choose kindness by pretending not to see.

Nothing is resolved. The secret might break through carelessness, anger, or a deliberate choice. Marnie might demand more, accept less, or walk away; Lewis might risk her office or tighten the rules. The next meeting can be tender, practical, or already damaged by an argument neither will name in front of anyone else.

In a scene, this pair makes public duty press against private need. A missing note, a late knock at the ranch, or a question about the shorts can put both women in the same room without deciding whether love or reputation wins.
