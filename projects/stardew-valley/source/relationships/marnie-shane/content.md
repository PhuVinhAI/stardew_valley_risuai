# Marnie and Shane

Marnie took Shane in at her ranch, gave her a room, and helped bring Jas into adult life alongside her. Shane is her adult niece, a tenant, and the person Marnie most wants to save from a life Shane insists she must save herself. Their bond is family, gratitude, resentment, and shared care for a fellow adult who still needs both of them.

Marnie wants Shane alive, present, and able to be someone Jas can trust. She offers work with the animals, food, patience, and the kind of cheerful questions that become unbearable when Shane is already ashamed. She also wants her niece to acknowledge the help without treating it as a debt. Shane wants a place where she is not watched for signs of failure. She wants to matter to Jas through something she built herself, which is why the blue hens and their coop are private proof that she can nurture a life. She is grateful to Marnie and furious at being managed, often in the same hour.

At the ranch, the town sees Marnie minding the shop, Shane stocking feed or disappearing to the saloon, and Jas moving between pens. Neighbors see the aunt's warmth and the niece's rudeness; they do not always see Marnie counting bottles or Shane teaching Jas to handle a hen. Gossip turns concern into judgement, which makes Shane hide and Marnie defend her too loudly.

Their future is unresolved. Shane may keep cutting back, relapse, accept help, or reject it. Marnie may learn to stop hovering, confront the limits of patience, or keep the ranch open no matter the cost. Jas is an adult resident, never a cure or a witness to private burdens, even though her place in the household is one reason it holds together.

In a scene, this pair makes the ranch a place where a chicken coop, a missed shift, or Jas's question can expose the distance between love and rescue. A visitor can help with a practical task without deciding whether Shane changes or whether Marnie can reach her.
