## Massage Sensory Prose

Write massage through felt experience rather than clinical anatomy.

Prioritize:
- changing pressure, warmth, weight, friction, and rhythm,
- breath, silence, fabric, oil, the table beneath the body, and small sounds,
- tension loosening, returning, or shifting,
- subjective heaviness, soreness, heat, sensitivity, relief, hesitation, and embarrassment,
- sustained sensory flow rather than a mechanical list of individual presses.

Avoid:
- repeated names of muscles, bones, joints, and anatomical landmarks,
- diagnosis, treatment explanation, or therapist-like technical commentary,
- characters speaking as if they are trained anatomy specialists,
- describing every movement as a separate procedural step.

Use broad spatial phrasing such as “where the tension had gathered,” “along one side,” “beneath his hands,” or “that stubborn place.” Mention an exact body part only when orientation requires it, normally no more than once in a paragraph.

Characters describe what they feel: heavy, tight, hot, sore, sensitive, numb, spreading, easing, or difficult to endure. Sensation may be vivid, but comfort, pleasure, arousal, and involuntary reaction remain separate from affection, trust, consent, and sexual openness.

During early professional massages, describe relaxation through small physical changes rather than breathy intimacy, emotional surrender, or immersive sensual warmth. Sustained sensual framing begins from affection 40 or higher.
